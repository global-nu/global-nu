<div class="stamp stamp--no">Nobel Prize in Physics</div>
<p>On 6 October the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin–Madison. <div class="stamp stamp--no">SBND detector paper</div>
<p>The SBND Collaboration published a paper describing the as-built detector and its first year of beam operation. SBND is a 112 tonne active-mass liquid argon time projection chamber in the Booster Neutrino Beam at Fermilab, part of the Short-Baseline Neutrino Program, and has operated since 2024. The paper reports that all detector systems performed at or above design requirements, with an electron lifetime exceeding 30 ms, ten times the design requirement, for more than 95% of Run 1.</p>

<div class="stamp stamp--no">PandaX-4T supernova background</div>
<p>PandaX-4T presents the first search for the diffuse supernova neutrino background using coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering. With a total exposure of 1.54 tonne·year from the commissioning and first science runs, it observed no significant event excess over the expected background. The collaboration sets a 90% confidence level upper limit of (2.27–2.43) × 10^3 cm^-2 s^-1 per flavour on the ν_x flux, for neutrino energies above 19.3 MeV.</p>

<div class="stamp stamp--no">Combined atmospheric fit</div>
<p>A preprint combines the latest publicly available atmospheric-neutrino data from Super-Kamiokande (all stages before the gadolinium phase), IceCube-DeepCore and the six-detection-unit configuration of KM3NeT/ORCA. All event samples are fitted simultaneously, with shared systematic uncertainties treated as fully correlated and detector-response uncertainties modelled independently for each experiment. The authors present the result as evidence for normal neutrino ordering and also quote constraints on the atmospheric oscillation parameters.</p>
<div class="cites"><span class="cite">Evidence for Normal Neutrino Ordering with Combined Atmospheric Neutrino Experiments — <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12208">arXiv</a></span></div>

<div class="stamp stamp--no">JUNO and an empirical relation</div>
<p>A single-author preprint takes the JUNO Collaboration's reported measurements of Δm²_21 and Δm²_31, which it describes as the most precise to date. It tests an empirical relation between the two mass-squared differences, proposed on empirical grounds, whose combination of square roots is predicted to equal √2. The author reports a value of 1.4143 (+0.0036, −0.0038), a relative precision of 0.27%, and concludes that the relation agrees excellently with the data. This is the author's interpretation, not a JUNO result.</p>
